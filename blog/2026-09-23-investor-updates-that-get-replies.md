A good investor update is one page, arrives on a predictable date, leads with the number that matters, and contains exactly one specific ask. It takes forty minutes to write and it is, per hour spent, the single highest-return activity available to a founder between rounds.

Most founders either do not send them, or send something that reads like a press release and receives no replies. This is the structure that works, and the tiering logic that lets you send to prospects and existing investors from the same source.

<!-- truncate -->

## Key takeaways

- **Monthly beats quarterly** at pre-seed and seed. Momentum is the product you are selling between rounds.
- Lead with the number. Not the narrative, not the thank-yous. The number.
- **One ask per update**, specific enough to action in under five minutes.
- Report bad news early and plainly. Investors who learn about a problem late stop trusting the good news too.
- Tier the content: prospective investors and committed investors should not receive identical detail.

## Why this is the highest-return hour in your month

Between rounds, an investor's entire model of your company is built from your updates. That is the whole dataset.

A founder who sends twelve consistent updates has demonstrated something no pitch can: that they say what they will do and then do it. When they open a round, they are not pitching to strangers. They are pitching to people who have watched them execute for a year.

Founders who go quiet for nine months and then send a deck are asking for a decision from someone with no basis for making one.

It also converts passes. A "too early for us" investor who receives your updates and watches you hit the milestone you named is the warmest lead you will ever have. This is why the pipeline treats passes as a nurture segment rather than a dead list, as covered in [run your raise as a pipeline](/blog/run-your-raise-as-a-pipeline-10-stages).

## The seven-block structure

**1. The headline number.** One line at the very top. "MRR $18,400, up 14% month over month." If revenue is not the number, pick the one that is and stay with it - changing your headline metric between updates is read, correctly, as hiding something.

**2. The metric table.** Four to six numbers, same ones every month, with the prior month and the change.

| Metric | Sep | Oct | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| MRR | $16,100 | $18,400 | +14% |
| Paying customers | 41 | 47 | +6 |
| Net revenue retention | 104% | 107% | +3pts |
| Cash | $412K | $371K | -$41K |
| Runway | 10.0 mo | 9.0 mo | -1.0 |

Include cash and runway. Founders leave them out when they are uncomfortable, which is precisely when investors most need to see them and most notice their absence.

**3. Wins.** Three bullets. Specific, with names and numbers. "Closed Acme on a $24K annual contract" beats "strong momentum in enterprise".

**4. Lowlights.** Two bullets. What went wrong and what you are doing about it.

This is the block that determines whether anyone believes the rest. An update with no lowlights is either dishonest or shows a founder not looking hard enough. Both are bad. Investors have read thousands of these and an all-good update is noted immediately.

**5. The ask.** Exactly one, specific, actionable in under five minutes:

> "Looking for an intro to a VP Engineering who has scaled a data platform team from 5 to 20. Anyone in your network?"

Not "let us know if you can help with hiring". A vague ask gets a vague non-answer. A specific ask gets a forwarded email.

**6. What is next.** Two or three lines on the next thirty days. This is what you will be measured against next month, which is the point.

**7. Housekeeping.** Team changes, fundraising status, anything material.

Total: one screen. If it needs scrolling twice, cut it.

## Tier the content, not the honesty

You are usually writing for two audiences with genuinely different entitlements:

| Block | Prospective investors | Committed investors |
|---|---|---|
| Headline number | Yes | Yes |
| Metrics | Growth rates and direction | Absolute numbers including cash and runway |
| Wins | Yes | Yes, with named customers |
| Lowlights | Summary | Full, with detail |
| Ask | Yes | Yes |
| Cap table changes | No | Yes |
| Fundraising detail | Status only | Terms, pipeline, timing |

The rule that keeps this honest: **tier the detail, never the substance.** Every audience gets the same picture of the business. Some get more granularity. If a prospective investor would be surprised by something a committed investor knows, you have crossed from tiering into misrepresentation.

## The mistakes that kill replies

**Inconsistent timing.** Pick a date - first Tuesday, say - and never move it. Predictability is the signal.

**Changing metrics.** If you reported MRR in March and "total users" in April, everyone assumes MRR went down.

**Burying the ask.** Paragraph six gets no replies.

**No bad news, ever.** Destroys credibility faster than the bad news would have.

**Attachments.** People read updates on their phone. Put it in the email body.

**Too long.** A two-page update gets skimmed. A one-page update gets read.

## When to start

Before you raise. A founder who can show six months of consistent updates during a fundraise has demonstrated an operating cadence that no slide can claim. It is one of the very few credible signals available to a pre-revenue company.

## Frequently asked questions

**How often should I send investor updates?**
Monthly at pre-seed and seed. Quarterly from Series A, if the board cadence covers the gap.

**What should be in a monthly investor update?**
A headline number, a consistent metric table including cash and runway, three wins, two lowlights, one specific ask, and what is next.

**Should I send updates to investors who passed?**
Yes. A pass is a "not now" far more often than a "never", and passes who watch you execute become your warmest leads.

**How long should an investor update be?**
One screen. Under 400 words.

**Should I include bad news in investor updates?**
Always. Investors who learn about problems late stop trusting the good news too, and early disclosure is when they can actually help.
