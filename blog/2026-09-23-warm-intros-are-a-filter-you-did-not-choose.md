"Get a warm intro" is the most repeated piece of fundraising advice and one of the least examined. It is presented as a quality filter. It is not. It is a **proximity filter**, and the thing it measures is how close you already are to capital.

If you are close, it works brilliantly. If you are not - wrong city, wrong school, first-generation founder, immigrant, building in a market the incumbents do not frequent - you are being filtered on a variable that has nothing to do with your company.

<!-- truncate -->

## Key takeaways

- The warm intro filters for **network proximity**, which, in our view, tracks geography and background more closely than company quality.
- It is rational for investors at the individual level and produces a systematically worse portfolio at the market level. See [adverse selection](/blog/adverse-selection-warm-intro-dealflow).
- Cold outreach works when the **evidence arrives with the message**, not after three emails.
- The strongest substitute for a network is a public, structured, self-serve record of your company.
- Build the surface before you need it. A raise is the worst time to start.

## What the warm intro is actually selecting for

The stated logic: an introducer's willingness to spend social capital is a signal of company quality.

The actual mechanics: the introducer's willingness is a function of how well they know you, how much they like you, and how little it costs them. All three correlate with proximity. None of them correlate strongly with whether the company will work.

The result is a filter that systematically selects for founders who went to certain universities, worked at certain companies, live in certain cities, and are demographically similar to the people who already hold capital. That is not a conspiracy. It is what happens when the routing mechanism is a personal network and nobody designed it for anything else.

For the founder, the practical consequence is simple and unfair: **the same company gets a different response depending on who forwards it.**

## Why investors use it anyway

It would be wrong to present this as laziness. The warm intro solves a real problem.

An investor receiving hundreds of inbound decks has no cheap way to tell which ones deserve an hour. Reading each one properly costs twenty minutes because every deck is a different shape. A warm intro cuts that cost to near zero by outsourcing triage to someone whose judgement they already trust.

So the warm intro is not a preference. It is a **response to the absence of a cheap, consistent screening mechanism.** Which tells you exactly what to do about it: the way to compete without a network is to make evaluating you cheap.

## The four things that substitute for a network

### 1. A public, structured record

The single highest-leverage asset. A permanent URL that contains your company in a standard, complete, readable form: team, product, problem, market, traction, what you are raising.

Not a deck attachment. A page that can be opened on a phone, at 11pm, by someone you have never met, and that answers their questions without a reply from you.

This does three jobs at once: it removes the reading cost that makes cold inbound unattractive, it is forwardable - so a partner can share you internally without a call - and it is crawlable, which means investors searching your category can find you. That last mechanic is covered in [your public startup profile is an SEO asset](/blog/public-startup-profile-seo-asset).

### 2. Cold outreach that carries its evidence

Most cold outreach fails for a structural reason, not a quality one: it asks for a conversation before providing any basis for wanting one.

The shape that works:

> **Subject: Customs automation for mid-market logistics - 47 customers, $18K MRR**
>
> Hi [name],
>
> You led [Company]'s seed round, which is the closest comparable to what we are building.
>
> We automate customs classification for mid-market logistics operators. 47 paying customers, $18K MRR, growing 14% monthly. The manual process we replace costs a typical customer 1.5 FTE.
>
> Full details, including metrics and cap table summary: [link]
>
> Raising $1.5M. Happy to talk if it is interesting.
>
> [name]

Four properties: a specific reason it is addressed to them, the numbers in the message rather than behind a request, a link that answers everything else, and no ask beyond a reply.

Response rates on this shape are not spectacular. They are non-zero, which is the relevant comparison against a warm intro you cannot obtain.

### 3. Being findable

Investors do search. They search when a thesis lands, when a portfolio company names a gap, when a category starts moving.

Being findable means: a public company page that search engines can read, a presence in investor-facing catalogues, and a founder who writes publicly about the problem. None of these produce a meeting next week. All of them compound, and all of them are unavailable to you if you start when the raise starts.

### 4. Earned proximity

The honest version: you can build a network, it just takes twelve to eighteen months and it is built by being useful, not by asking.

Write publicly about your problem space with actual insight. Answer questions where founders and investors are already talking. Introduce other founders to each other. Share what you are learning, including the failures.

None of this is a fundraising tactic and it does not work if executed as one. It works because eighteen months of visible competence is itself a signal, and it converts the cold intro into a warm one without ever asking anyone for a favour.

## What to actually do

Use warm intros where you have them. They work, and refusing them on principle is a tax you pay for nothing.

But treat them as a bonus channel rather than the strategy, and build the three structural assets - a public record, a cold outreach motion, and findability - because they are the only parts of the process you control.

Then track the difference. Log every investor contact with its channel, and compare conversion by channel. Many founders find their cold-outreach conversion is lower than their warm-intro conversion but their cold-outreach **volume** is far higher, which can make it the larger contributor to the funnel.

That is a fact you can only learn if you run both. Which is the argument for running both.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do cold emails to VCs work?**
Yes, at a lower conversion rate than warm intros and at much higher volume. They work best when the numbers are in the message and a complete record is one click away.

**How do I get a warm intro to an investor?**
Portfolio founders are the best route, followed by shared advisors and prior colleagues. Make it easy: send a forwardable paragraph plus a link, never a request to "have a chat about intros".

**Is it possible to raise without a network?**
Yes, and harder. The substitute is being cheap to evaluate: a public structured record, specific cold outreach, and being findable in the places investors look.

**What should I send in a cold email to an investor?**
A specific reason you are writing to them, your key numbers in the body, and one link to a complete record. No attachments.
