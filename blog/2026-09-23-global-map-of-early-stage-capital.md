In the first quarter of 2026, about 83% of global startup capital went to US companies. By the second quarter it was roughly two thirds (Crunchbase News).

That is a very large move in three months. Part of it is US deployment falling back from an exceptional first quarter; part of it is everyone else accelerating. This is where early-stage capital is actually forming, and what it changes for a founder raising across a border.

<!-- truncate -->

## Key takeaways

- **US share fell from ~83% to ~two thirds** between Q1 and Q2 2026, as US deployment fell back from a record Q1 and other regions grew.
- **Europe posted its strongest venture quarter in four years**, with UK companies raising over $10 billion in Q2 alone.
- **China's quarterly total rose sharply**, and Singapore and India each raised a few billion dollars.
- Cross-border rounds lose every informal shortcut: no shared network, legal defaults, timezone or format.
- The practical requirement is a **self-serve record**. A distant investor will not chase you for what is missing.

## The numbers

Global startup investment reached roughly **$510 billion in H1 2026** (Crunchbase), with about $305 billion in Q1 and $205 billion in Q2.

| Region | H1 2026 signal |
|---|---|
| **US and Canada** | ~$392B, up sharply year over year. Roughly two thirds of Q2 global capital, down from ~83% of a much larger Q1. |
| **Europe** | Strongest venture quarter in four years. UK companies raised over $10B in Q2. |
| **China** | Up very sharply year over year and from Q1. |
| **Singapore** | A few billion dollars |
| **India** | A few billion dollars |

Two caveats that matter for reading this correctly.

**The headline is distorted by AI.** Over 70% of Q2 global capital went to AI companies (Crunchbase News), up from just under 50% a year earlier, and most of it went into large late-stage rounds. Aggregate regional totals are heavily influenced by a small number of very large deals, which is not the market a pre-seed founder operates in.

**Quarterly regional shares are volatile.** A single $10 billion round moves a country's share materially. Read the direction, not the decimal.

## What is actually changing

Three structural shifts sit under the quarterly noise.

**1. Local capital is forming where it did not exist.** The Gulf, Southeast Asia and parts of Central and Eastern Europe have built domestic early-stage ecosystems over the last five years - local funds, local angels, local accelerators. Founders in those markets increasingly raise their first round locally rather than relocating.

**2. Cross-border investing became normal.** Remote diligence, which was an emergency adaptation in 2020, is now the default. An investor taking a first call with a founder three time zones away is unremarkable.

**3. The talent-capital link loosened.** Companies build distributed teams from day one. The historical logic that pulled companies to a single city - proximity to capital and engineers - weakened on both sides.

Net effect: capital formation is globalising faster than investor networks are. Which creates a specific and exploitable gap.

## The gap worth exploiting

An investor's network is local, almost by definition. Capital and opportunity are increasingly not.

That means a growing share of the good opportunity set sits outside any given investor's warm-intro range. For investors, that is the [adverse selection problem](/blog/adverse-selection-warm-intro-dealflow) getting structurally worse each quarter.

For founders outside the main hubs, it is the opposite: the pool of investors who will seriously consider a company they cannot get to in an afternoon is larger every year. The constraint is no longer their willingness. It is whether you are **findable and evaluable** from a distance.

## What a cross-border raise actually requires

A domestic raise runs on informal shortcuts. A cross-border raise loses all of them at once.

| Shortcut | Domestic | Cross-border |
|---|---|---|
| Reference checks | One call to a mutual contact | No mutual contacts. Days of work. |
| Legal review | Familiar templates, known defaults | Unfamiliar entity type, unknown norms |
| Quick clarification | A text, answered in an hour | An email, answered tomorrow |
| Reading your materials | Familiar format, local conventions | No shared conventions at all |
| Meeting | Coffee | A scheduled call across eight time zones |

Every one of those costs days instead of minutes. Four of the five are fixed by the same thing: a complete, structured, self-serve record.

The blunt version: **a distant investor will not chase you.** A local investor who cannot find your cap table sends a text. A distant investor who cannot find it deprioritises you and moves to the next company, and you never learn why.

## The practical checklist for raising across a border

1. **Entity clarity.** State your jurisdiction, entity type and where your IP sits, prominently. A US investor looking at an Estonian OÜ or a UK Ltd needs thirty seconds, not a call.
2. **Currency discipline.** Pick one reporting currency and state it everywhere. Mixed-currency metrics are read as careless.
3. **Comparable metrics.** Define every metric explicitly. Conventions differ by market, and an undefined number is discounted.
4. **Flip readiness.** If a US round will require a Delaware flip, say so and show you understand the cost and timeline. Silence on this reads as naivety.
5. **A public, crawlable profile.** Distant investors find companies by searching. See [your public startup profile is an SEO asset](/blog/public-startup-profile-seo-asset).
6. **Same-day responsiveness.** With a timezone gap, a one-day delay is a two-day round trip. Answer before you sleep.

## For investors

The corollary is that a network-bound sourcing strategy is sampling from a shrinking share of the global opportunity set, and the share shrinks every quarter.

Widening geographically has exactly one prerequisite: screening a company from an unfamiliar market must be cheap. That means structured intake, because the reassembly cost that makes unstructured screening expensive is worse across borders, not better - you cannot even rely on familiar conventions to orient yourself.

## Frequently asked questions

**Which region gets the most venture capital?**
North America, by a wide margin, though its share fell from roughly 83% to about two thirds of global capital between Q1 and Q2 2026.

**Is European venture capital growing?**
Yes. Europe posted its strongest venture quarter in four years in Q2 2026, with UK companies raising over $10 billion in that quarter alone.

**Can I raise from US investors as a non-US company?**
Yes, and it is increasingly common. Expect questions about entity structure and a possible Delaware flip, and prepare a complete self-serve record because distant investors will not chase missing information.

**Do I need to relocate to raise venture capital?**
Far less than five years ago. Remote diligence is standard and local capital exists in many more markets.
