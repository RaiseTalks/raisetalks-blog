A fundraising readiness score is a structured assessment of how prepared your company is to be evaluated by an investor: not how good your business is, but how legible it is. Every investor already does this informally, in the first few minutes, from whatever you have sent them. The only question is whether you know your score before they form it.

This is the framework, what each level actually means, and the specific work that moves you up one.

<!-- truncate -->

## Key takeaways

- Readiness is **not** the same as quality. Excellent companies score badly, and that costs them real money and real months.
- The working framework has four dimensions: **Team, Product, Market, Deal**, each scored one to five.
- A 3.0 average is the practical floor for sharing your record widely. Below that you are burning first impressions.
- The score moves fastest through **evidence and specificity**, not through traction. Most of the gap is writing that has not been done.
- A score is only useful if it names the gap and links to the fix. A number with no next action is a vanity metric.

## Why a score exists at all

An investor seeing forty companies a month has to triage. Triage needs a comparable signal, and in the absence of a real one they use proxies: how well-known the referrer is, how polished the deck is, whether the founder is from a recognisable company.

Those proxies are cheap, fast and systematically biased. They select for network access rather than company quality, which is precisely why so much good dealflow never gets read. We go through the economics of that in [adverse selection](/blog/adverse-selection-warm-intro-dealflow).

A readiness score is an attempt to replace a proxy with a measurement. It answers a narrow, honest question: **based on what this company has actually documented, how much of a serious evaluation can be completed right now?**

That is a lower bar than "is this a good investment." It is also the bar that determines whether anyone gets far enough to ask the second question.

## The four dimensions

### Team (1-5)

What is being assessed: whether the people are identifiable, credible and appropriately assembled for this specific problem.

| Level | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| 1 | Names and titles. Nothing verifiable. |
| 2 | Bios exist. Generic. No link between background and this problem. |
| 3 | Specific, verifiable backgrounds. Roles and ownership are clear. |
| 4 | Above, plus demonstrated founder-problem fit and a stated reason this team wins. |
| 5 | Above, plus prior relevant outcomes, named advisors, and an honest statement of the gap the round hires against. |

The cheapest upgrade in the entire framework lives here. Most teams sit at 2 because nobody wrote two sentences explaining why *these* people are attacking *this* problem.

### Product (1-5)

What is being assessed: whether something exists, what state it is in, and whether a technical reader can sanity-check it.

The jump from 2 to 4 is usually a plain-language description plus an honest shipped-versus-roadmap split. Founders lose points for overclaiming far more often than for having built little. An investor can fund an early product. They cannot fund a product they suspect is being misrepresented.

### Market (1-5)

What is being assessed: whether you have defined a buyer, sized the opportunity with a method, named your competitors and stated a differentiation you would defend.

The most common cap on this dimension is a market size with no derivation. If a partner cannot reconstruct your number, it is not a number, and a 5 is impossible no matter how large it is.

### Deal (1-5)

What is being assessed: whether there is a coherent transaction on the table.

Amount, instrument, valuation or cap, use of funds, the milestones the money buys, current cap table including SAFEs and notes, burn and runway. Founders routinely sit at 1 or 2 here deep into a process, which is why "interested" so often fails to become a term sheet. See [the readiness gap](/blog/readiness-gap-interested-to-term-sheet).

## What the composite score means

| Average | Reading | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| **Below 2.5** | Not evaluable. An investor cannot form a view. | Do not share the link. Fix the record first. |
| **2.5 - 3.0** | Partially evaluable. Reading it costs the investor work. | Close the single lowest dimension before any outreach. |
| **3.0 - 3.75** | Evaluable. A partner can reach a considered view unaided. | Share widely. Work the next weakest dimension in parallel. |
| **3.75 - 4.5** | Strong. Diligence starts from confirmation, not discovery. | Push on evidence depth and third-party verification. |
| **Above 4.5** | Rare. Usually means real traction plus a disciplined record. | Use it. Lead with the score. |

**3.0 is the practical floor for outreach.** Below it you are spending non-renewable first impressions on a version of your company that does not represent it. ## The thing that actually moves the score

Founders assume the answer is traction. Usually it is not.

Sort the typical gap list by how much score it unlocks per hour of work:

1. **Write the team narrative.** Two sentences per founder linking background to this problem. One hour. Often worth a full point.
2. **State your market method.** Show the derivation, not the total. One hour. Frequently a full point.
3. **Split shipped from roadmap.** An honest table. Ninety minutes. Removes the credibility discount.
4. **Define every metric.** What counts as active, over what window, measured how. Two hours. Prevents the quiet disqualification nobody tells you about.
5. **Reconcile the cap table** against every SAFE and note. Half a day. As a rule of thumb, without it the deal dimension rarely gets past 2.
6. **Write the use of funds against milestones.** "18 months of runway" is not a use of funds. "$400K to ship X, $600K to hire two AEs, target $40K MRR by month 14" is.

All six are writing tasks. None require a single new customer. That is the uncomfortable and useful finding: **most readiness gaps are documentation gaps wearing the costume of traction gaps.**

## Scoring yourself honestly

Two rules make self-assessment work:

**Score the record, not your intent.** The question is never "do we know this?" It is "is this written down where an investor will find it?" Knowledge that lives in the founder's head scores zero.

**Have someone else score it.** In our experience, founders tend to over-score Product and under-score Deal. Give the link to an advisor, ask them to score blind, and compare. The gaps between your score and theirs are the real list.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is a good fundraising readiness score?**
On a five-point scale, 3.0 is the floor for widely sharing your record and 3.75 or above is genuinely strong. Below 2.5 an investor cannot form a view at all.

**How is investor readiness measured?**
Across four dimensions - team, product, market and deal - each scored on the evidence you have documented, not on the quality of the underlying business.

**Can I raise with a low readiness score?**
Yes, with a warm introduction and a patient investor. You will raise slower, at worse terms, from a smaller pool. Readiness does not determine whether you can raise; it determines the cost.

**Does readiness scoring replace a pitch?**
No. It determines whether your pitch gets a serious reading. The score gets you evaluated; the company gets you funded.

**How long does it take to move from 2.5 to 3.5?**
For most companies, under a week of focused writing. The gap is almost always documentation, not traction.
