Investors search. They search when a thesis crystallises, when a portfolio company names a gap, when a category starts moving, when a partner asks who else is doing this.

When they search, most startups are unfindable. A homepage optimised to sell to customers, a LinkedIn page with 40 followers, and a deck that exists only as an email attachment. None of that answers an investor's question, and none of it is indexed against the terms they use.

<!-- truncate -->

## Key takeaways

- Your marketing site sells to **customers**. It rarely answers an investor's questions.
- A public company profile is a **crawlable, forwardable, permanent** answer to "who is doing X".
- Target investor-intent long-tail phrases, not your product category head term.
- **Forwardability** may matter more than search: a partner sharing you internally needs a link that works without you.
- This compounds slowly. Build it eighteen months before you need it, not during the raise.

## What investors actually search for

Not "best CRM software". They search like allocators:

- "customs automation startup seed"
- "pre-seed logistics SaaS Europe"
- "startups building agentic workflow tools"
- "[category] companies raising 2026"
- "[competitor name] alternative startup"

These are long-tail, low-volume, extremely high-intent queries. Nobody is competing for them because most companies optimise for buyer intent, which is the right call for revenue and leaves the investor-intent surface almost empty.

Low volume matters less than it sounds. You do not need a thousand visitors. You need the four partners researching your category this quarter to find you.

## Why your marketing site does not do this job

It is built to convert a buyer. It leads with outcomes and benefits, hides the mechanism, and never mentions the things an investor needs:

- Stage and what you are raising
- Founding team with real backgrounds
- Market sizing with a method
- Traction with definitions
- Named competitors and your differentiation

A buyer does not want any of that. An investor wants all of it. Making one page serve both produces a page that serves neither, which is why the answer is a **separate, investor-facing profile** rather than a rewrite of your homepage.

## What a strong investor profile contains

1. **A one-line description** with the category in plain language, not in positioning language. "Customs classification automation for mid-market logistics" beats "the intelligence layer for global trade".
2. **The problem, specifically**, with the cost of the status quo quantified.
3. **The team**, with real backgrounds linked to this problem.
4. **The product**, honestly split into shipped and planned.
5. **The market**, sized bottom-up with the arithmetic visible.
6. **Traction**, with every metric defined.
7. **Competitors**, named, with your differentiation stated as a defensible claim.
8. **The raise**, at a headline level: stage, amount, what it buys.

Public tier only. Nothing here is sensitive, and treating it as such is what keeps most companies invisible. Cap table, detailed financials and customer contracts belong at a [restricted tier](/blog/data-room-access-tiers-what-to-share).

## The technical requirements

Four things, and most startups miss at least two:

**A crawlable URL.** Server-rendered, not behind a login, not a JavaScript app that returns an empty shell to a crawler. Test it: `curl` the page and check whether your company description is in the HTML.

**Open Graph tags.** When someone drops your link in a Slack or a WhatsApp thread, the preview is the first impression. A blank preview reads as an unfinished company.

**Structured data.** JSON-LD `Organization` markup so search engines and AI tools can parse what your company is, rather than inferring it.

**Genuine text content.** Not an image of a deck. Not a PDF embed. Actual indexable prose containing the words an investor would search.

## Forwardability beats search

Here is the underrated mechanic, and it works from day one rather than compounding over months.

Most investor discovery is not a search result. It is a forward. An associate finds you, sends the link to a partner. A partner shares it in the Monday meeting. An angel forwards it to a syndicate. A founder mentions you to their own investor.

Every one of those is a moment where a link either works or does not. A link to a deck attachment does not survive a forward. A link that requires a login does not survive a forward. A permanent public page does.

Which means the question is not only "can investors find me" but **"when someone wants to show me to someone else, what do they send?"** If the answer is "an email with an attachment", you are invisible in exactly the moment that matters most.

## The timing problem

This compounds. A page indexed today typically takes months to rank. A profile that has existed for two years with accumulated links ranks far better than one created last week.

Which means the correct time to build it is well before you need it. A founder who publishes a profile eighteen months before a raise has an asset. A founder who publishes one the week they start raising has a URL.

The good news is the cost: one afternoon, once, then updates alongside your other materials.

## Measuring it

Three things worth tracking:

- **Impressions and clicks** on investor-intent queries, via Search Console.
- **Referral sources.** Where forwards actually come from.
- **Inbound investor contacts** that cite the profile. The only metric that matters.

Expect approximately nothing for the first three months. This is a compounding asset, and the founders who abandon it at week six are the reason it stays uncontested.

## Frequently asked questions

**How do investors find startups?**
Referrals first, then investor-facing catalogues, then search when researching a specific thesis or category.

**Should startups do SEO?**
For customers, depending on the business. For investors, yes - the investor-intent long tail is high-value and almost entirely uncontested.

**What should be on a startup's investor page?**
One-line description, problem, team, product state, market with a method, defined traction metrics, named competitors, and the raise at a headline level.

**Is it risky to publish company information publicly?**
Not at the public tier. Nothing there is sensitive. The risk is in publishing cap table and financial detail, which belongs behind tiered access.

**How long does it take for a profile to rank?**
Typically months to begin ranking, longer to rank well. Build it before you need it.
