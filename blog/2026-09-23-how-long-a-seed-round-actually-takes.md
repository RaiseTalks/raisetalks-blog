A seed round in 2026 takes **three to six months end to end**. Inside that, the active window from first outreach to signed term sheet runs six to fourteen weeks, and term sheet to money in the bank adds another four to eight.

Founders plan for eight weeks. That gap - between a three-to-six-month reality and a two-month plan - is the source of most of the bad decisions made during a raise.

<!-- truncate -->

## Key takeaways

- **Three to six months**, start to wire. Plan six.
- The **lead investor is the variable that matters.** Founders who land a lead in the first four weeks close materially faster.
- Preparation is not part of the clock, and it is where the time is actually won or lost.
- Diligence on a priced round adds two to four weeks on its own.
- Start your raise with **9 to 12 months of cash**. Anything less and you negotiate from weakness.

---

## The phases

| Phase | Duration | What is happening |
|---|---|---|
| **0. Preparation** | 2-6 weeks | Data room, model, cap table, target list, warm paths. Off the clock, but determines everything after. |
| **1. Outreach** | 2-4 weeks | First contacts, intro requests, calls booked |
| **2. First meetings** | 3-5 weeks | Typically a few dozen first calls, running in parallel |
| **3. Second meetings and deep dives** | 2-4 weeks | Partner meetings, technical reviews, customer calls |
| **4. Lead conversion** | 2-6 weeks | Converting interest into a written offer. Highest variance. |
| **5. Diligence** | 2-4 weeks | Verification, references, legal review |
| **6. Docs and close** | 2-4 weeks | Legal drafting, signatures, wires |

Phases 1 to 3 overlap heavily and together take about 4-8 weeks. Phases 4 to 6 largely do not, which is why the back half feels so slow after a fast start.

**Total: 10 to 22 weeks from first outreach to wire, or 12 to 28 weeks including preparation.** Roughly three to six months.

## The single variable that matters

Among everything a founder controls, one thing dominates the timeline: **how quickly you land a lead.**

Founders who secure a lead investor within the first four weeks of active outreach close substantially faster than those who do not. The mechanism is not mysterious. A lead sets the terms, does the deep diligence, and creates the deadline that makes every other investor decide. Without a lead, twenty interested parties are twenty people waiting to see what happens.

The practical implication: **front-load your best-fit, most-likely-lead investors.** The common advice to "practise on your B-list first" is expensive. It spends your freshest weeks on conversations that cannot produce a lead, and by the time you reach your A-list you are six weeks in with no momentum to show.

## Why founders underestimate by half

Four consistent reasons:

**They count from first meeting, not from preparation.** The two to six weeks of getting the data room and model in order is real time, and it is time when nothing else is progressing.

**They assume sequential, get parallel-then-serial.** The first three phases run beautifully in parallel and it feels fast. Then phase 4 begins and everything becomes serial: one lead, one partner meeting cycle, one legal process.

**They underestimate the close.** Term sheet to wire is four to eight weeks, covering diligence and docs, and it is entirely at the mercy of two law firms' capacity and a signature from a person on holiday.

**They forget August and December.** Fundraising stops. A round that would take four months takes six if it spans either.

## What actually slows it down

In rough order of frequency:

1. **Incomplete data room.** Every question answered by email instead of by the record costs two to four days of calendar time. Twenty such questions is six weeks.
2. **No lead.** Discussed above. The dominant factor.
3. **Cap table problems.** Unrecorded SAFEs, unpapered advisor equity, missing IP assignment. Always discovered in diligence, always at the worst moment.
4. **Metrics that do not survive verification.** A number in the deck that does not match the number in the system restarts trust from zero.
5. **Too few investors in the funnel.** Twenty contacts cannot produce a competitive process, and a non-competitive process has no deadline.
6. **The founder's own responsiveness.** A 48-hour reply time during diligence adds weeks across a round.

Item 1 is worth dwelling on because it is the most under-rated. A data room that answers questions before they are asked does not save an hour, it removes an entire round-trip cycle from the calendar. Across a whole raise, dozens of avoidable round trips can be the difference between a four-month and a six-month process. That is the economics behind [the data room, field by field](/blog/startup-data-room-field-by-field-2026).

## When to start

**With 9 to 12 months of cash.**

The arithmetic: six months to raise, plus a margin for the round that does not come together first time. A founder starting with six months of runway is negotiating with a visible deadline, and every investor can see it. Terms get worse, and a re-price at month five is very hard to refuse.

A founder starting with twelve months can walk away from a bad term sheet. That option is worth more than any negotiation tactic.

## Compressing it

You cannot compress phases 5 and 6 much - they run at the speed of lawyers. What you can compress:

- **Preparation**, by having done it already. A maintained record is not a fundraising task.
- **Phase 4**, by front-loading likely leads and running a genuinely parallel process.
- **The gaps between phases**, by answering same-day and having the answer already written.

The realistic best case for a well-prepared founder with a strong company is around ten to twelve weeks from first outreach to wire. That is fast, and it is not achievable by working harder during the round. It is achieved by the work done before it started.

## Method and sources

Phase durations are our planning estimates, informed by venture process guidance and cross-referenced with Carta and PitchBook data on inter-round gaps and deal velocity. Individual rounds vary widely by sector, geography and whether a lead is secured early. Treat these as planning ranges, not benchmarks.

## Frequently asked questions

**How long does it take to raise a seed round?**
Three to six months from the start of preparation to money in the bank. Plan for six.

**How long is due diligence for a seed round?**
Two to four weeks for a priced round; less for a SAFE. Including docs and close, term sheet to wire takes four to eight weeks.

**When should I start raising?**
With 9 to 12 months of cash left.

**How many investor meetings does a seed round take?**
As a rough illustration, a few dozen first meetings from around a hundred contacts, converting to one lead. It varies widely.

**What is the fastest a seed round can close?**
Ten to twelve weeks for a well-prepared founder with a strong company and an early lead. Faster than that usually means a pre-existing relationship.
