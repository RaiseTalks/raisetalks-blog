A fundraising round is a sales pipeline. One product, one buyer segment, a long cycle, a hard deadline and a conversion rate that is worse than you expect. Founders who have run sales accept this immediately. Founders who have not tend to run their raise as a list of relationships and are then surprised when the round runs to the long end of the three-to-six-month range, or beyond it.

This is the ten-stage structure, the objective gate condition for each stage, and the three metrics that tell you whether the round is actually moving.

<!-- truncate -->

## Key takeaways

- Stages must be defined by **what has happened**, not by how the conversation felt. "Warm" is not a stage.
- You need **three to five times more investors in the funnel** than you think, because top-of-funnel conversion at seed is brutal.
- The two metrics that predict a closed round are **stage-to-stage conversion** and **time in stage**. Not the number of meetings.
- A stalled deal is not a lost deal, but it is not an open one either. Force it to a terminal state.
- The whole system runs on one thing: a single record of your company that every stage can point at.

## Why a list of names is not a pipeline

The default founder system is a spreadsheet of investor names with a column called "status" containing values like "warm", "interested", "following up" and "met at conference".

None of those are states. They are feelings. A pipeline built on feelings cannot tell you conversion rates, cannot tell you which stage is leaking, and cannot tell you whether you will close in six weeks or never. It also systematically over-reports health, because "interested" persists long after interest has died.

A stage is a state with an **objective entry condition**. Something observable happened. That is the only requirement, and it changes everything downstream.

## The ten stages

| # | Stage | Gate condition (what must have happened) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Data room shared** | They have access to your record. Not "I sent the deck". Access granted. |
| 2 | **NDA signed** | Where applicable. Many early-stage investors will not sign one; do not let this block you. |
| 3 | **First call** | A scheduled call has happened. Not booked. Happened. |
| 4 | **Second call** | A follow-up with more of their team, or a deep-dive on one dimension. |
| 5 | **Due diligence** | They have asked for specific verification: customer references, financial detail, technical review. |
| 6 | **Term sheet** | A written offer exists, however informal. |
| 7 | **Soft commitment** | Verbal yes on an amount, subject to conditions. |
| 8 | **Hard commitment** | Signed documents. Money is contractually promised. |
| - | **On hold** | Explicitly parked with a date to revisit. |
| - | **Rejected** | A pass, recorded, with the reason. |

Two things about this list are commonly resisted.

**Stage 5 is a real stage, not a formality.** The move from second call to diligence is the single highest-signal transition in the entire funnel. An investor who asks for customer references is spending their own time. That is worth more than any amount of enthusiasm.

**Rejected needs a recorded reason.** Fifteen passes with reasons is the most valuable dataset you will generate during your raise. Fifteen passes with no reasons is fifteen units of nothing. If three investors independently pass on the same dimension, that is not bad luck. That is a finding about your company.

## How many investors you actually need

Rough seed-stage shape, and it is worse than most founders plan for:

| Stage | Indicative count | Conversion to next |
|---|---|---|
| Identified and researched | 150 | - |
| Contacted | 100 | 67% |
| First call | 30 | 30% |
| Second call | 12 | 40% |
| Diligence | 6 | 50% |
| Term sheet | 2 | 33% |
| Closed lead | 1 | 50% |

These are indicative, not benchmarks, and they vary enormously by sector, geography and warmth of channel. The point is the shape. **A hundred contacts to one lead.** Founders who build a list of twenty investors are not running a narrow, efficient process. They are running a process that mathematically cannot close.

The corollary matters too: if you are going to have a hundred first contacts, the cost of preparing each one has to be near zero. That is only true if there is one canonical record you share, rather than a bespoke package assembled per investor.

## The three metrics that matter

**1. Stage-to-stage conversion.** Where is the leak? A 5% first-call rate is a targeting or outreach problem. A 30% first-call rate that produces no second calls is a story or evidence problem. A healthy top of funnel that dies in diligence is a record problem: your claims are not surviving verification.

**2. Time in stage.** Every deal has a half-life. A company sitting in "first call" for five weeks is not in your pipeline, it is in your imagination. Set a maximum per stage: seven days post-first-call, fourteen in diligence, ten from term sheet. Past that, one direct email, then move it to on hold with a date.

**3. Concentration risk.** If 70% of your projected round sits with one investor, you do not have a round. You have a single point of failure with a good attitude. Track the share of your target held by your top prospect, and keep working the funnel until a lead is signed.

## The operating cadence

A weekly thirty-minute review of your own pipeline, with three questions:

1. What moved stage this week, and why?
2. What has been in the same stage past its limit?
3. What is the single next action for every deal past stage 3, with a date?

Everything else - the notes, the intro requests, the follow-ups - hangs off that.

Between reviews, the most valuable habit is logging the conversation the same day. Not a transcript. Three lines: what they asked about, what they were sceptical of, what they committed to do next. Across forty conversations that becomes a map of where your story is weakest, which no single conversation would ever reveal.

## Investor updates are part of the pipeline

The founders who close fastest treat the "no for now" investors as a nurture segment, not as a dead list. A monthly update to everyone at stage 3 or beyond is the single highest-return activity in a raise. It converts "too early for us" into "you have shipped exactly what you said you would", which is the most persuasive thing a founder can demonstrate.

## What the pipeline runs on

One structured record of your company, shared at the right access tier per stage:

- Stages 1-3: public or basic tier. Enough to evaluate, nothing sensitive.
- Stages 4-5: basic, moving to due-diligence tier as they engage.
- Stages 6-8: full due-diligence access.

This is why [tiered access](/blog/data-room-access-tiers-what-to-share) and a pipeline are the same system rather than two tools. The stage determines the tier; the tier makes the stage possible without a separate negotiation.

## Frequently asked questions

**How many investors should I contact for a seed round?**
Plan on roughly 100 contacted to produce one lead. Build a researched list of 150 or more before you start.

**What CRM should I use for fundraising?**
Anything you will actually update. A spreadsheet with real stage definitions beats a sophisticated CRM you abandon in week three. A purpose-built fundraising pipeline wins because the stages and the company record are already connected.

**How long should an investor stay in one stage?**
As a rule of thumb: seven days after a first call, fourteen in diligence, ten from term sheet to signature. Past that, ask directly, then move them to on hold.

**Should I tell investors where they are in my process?**
Yes, factually. "We are taking first calls through the 20th and aiming to close by mid-December" is useful information, not pressure. Manufactured urgency is transparent and damages you.

**What do I do with a pass?**
Record the reason, thank them, and add them to your monthly update list. Passes at pre-seed frequently become checks at seed.
