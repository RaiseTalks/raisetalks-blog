An angel or solo GP has the same job as a fund with fifteen people and none of the leverage. No analyst to screen, no associate to take reference calls, no platform team to maintain a CRM. Often it is not even a full-time role.

The instinct is to compensate with judgement and a narrow network. That produces a small, homogeneous portfolio and a process that quietly degrades whenever the week gets busy. This is the alternative: a system sized for one person.

<!-- truncate -->

## Key takeaways

- Budget **four hours a week**, fixed, and design the process to fit inside it.
- The constraint is screening cost. Solve that and everything else becomes affordable.
- **Never delegate or automate**: founder reference calls, the thesis, the decision.
- Syndicates work when the division of labour is explicit, and fail when everyone assumes someone else did diligence.
- Write one page per decision, including passes. It is the only feedback loop you have.

---

## The four-hour week

| Block | Time | Job |
|---|---|---|
| **Screen** | 60 min, once | Everything new since last week, thesis-fit then quality |
| **Calls** | 90 min | Two founder calls, 45 minutes each |
| **Diligence** | 60 min | Reference calls and verification on live deals |
| **Admin** | 30 min | Update pipeline, write memos, log passes |

Four hours, on the same day every week. The fixed slot matters more than the total: a process that only runs when you have spare capacity is a process that stops in month three, and the companies that arrive during your busy weeks are not systematically worse than the ones that arrive during your quiet weeks.

## Make screening cheap or nothing else works

For a solo investor, screening is the binding constraint. At twenty minutes per company, sixty minutes covers three companies a week. That is 150 a year, which is not enough funnel to build a portfolio and forces you straight back onto warm intros - with all the [costs that carries](/blog/adverse-selection-warm-intro-dealflow).

Three levers:

**1. Publish a specific thesis.** Ten lines: sector, stage, geography, cheque size, hard exclusions. Put it on your profile and your site. It pre-filters inbound before it reaches you, which is the cheapest screening available because someone else does it.

**2. Use structured sources.** A company arriving as a standard field set takes ninety seconds to screen. The same company as a deck takes twenty minutes. That single difference turns 150 companies a year into well over 1,000.

**3. Apply a written gate.** Four or five binary questions, applied identically. Judgement belongs in the 45-minute call, not in the 90-second screen. See [pre-screening at scale](/blog/pre-screening-at-scale-scoring-startups).

## What to automate, and what never to

| Automate | Never automate |
|---|---|
| Extracting facts from a company record | Founder reference calls |
| Normalising metrics into comparable units | The investment thesis |
| Flagging missing or contradictory information | The decision |
| Drafting the memo skeleton | Judging the team |
| Pipeline reminders and stage tracking | Deciding what risk you accept |

AI is genuinely good at the left column and produces a reliable first-pass summary from a structured record. It removes the blank-page cost that stops most solo investors writing memos at all.

It is not good at the right column, and the accountability for those cannot be delegated anyway. An AI-generated thesis reads plausibly and commits to nothing, which is the opposite of what a thesis is for.

## Proportionate diligence for a solo investor

The [full pre-seed checklist](/blog/proportionate-diligence-pre-seed-checklist) is a week of work. For a $25K angel cheque, compress to the four non-negotiables plus two conversations:

1. **Entity and good standing.** Five minutes, registry search.
2. **Cap table reconciles.** Fifteen minutes with the founder's own table.
3. **IP assignment confirmed.** One question, one document. The most commonly skipped and most expensive omission.
4. **Two founder references.** One hour. The highest-value hour in the process.
5. **One customer conversation.** Thirty minutes.
6. **One metric verified at source.** Thirty minutes.

Under three hours. It catches the structural problems that are genuinely fatal and skips everything that cannot yet be known.

The temptation is to skip steps 1 to 3 because they feel like paperwork and you trust the founder. They are not about trust. Founders lose track of their own cap tables routinely and rarely know whether a contractor from 2024 signed an assignment.

## Syndicates: make the division of labour explicit

Syndicates are the natural leverage for angels, and they fail in a specific, predictable way: **everyone assumes someone else did the work.**

The lead is presumed to have done diligence. The lead presumes the members will do their own. Nobody calls a reference. The round closes on collective enthusiasm.

The fix is a stated split, written into the deal note:

| Role | Owns |
|---|---|
| **Lead** | Entity, cap table, IP, founder references, the memo |
| **Domain member** | The technical or market-specific verification |
| **Members** | Read the memo, ask one hard question each, decide independently |

And one rule that does most of the work: **the memo states what was verified and what was not.** A member reading "IP assignment confirmed; customer references not taken" can make an informed decision. A member reading "diligence complete" cannot.

## Write one page per decision

Including passes. Especially passes.

Your investments are a biased sample of companies you liked. Your passes are the full distribution, and they are where any pattern in your judgement is actually visible. If you keep passing on a founder profile that keeps working, the pass archive is the only place you will ever see it.

Pass memos can be three lines: company, category, one-sentence reason. Use a controlled vocabulary - out of thesis, team gap, market too small, no demand evidence, deal structure, valuation, too early, cannot verify - so that after a hundred passes you can count them.

Almost no solo investor keeps this archive. It is the cheapest edge available.

## What the system buys you

Run this for a year and you will have: a specific thesis that filters inbound, several hundred companies screened rather than a few dozen, a written record of every decision and its reasoning, and a portfolio that is not simply a map of your existing network.

The last one is the point. A solo investor's structural disadvantage is not capital or brand. It is that an unsystematic process defaults to proximity, and proximity is not a strategy.

## Frequently asked questions

**How much diligence should an angel do?**
Under three hours for a small cheque: entity, cap table, IP assignment, two founder references, one customer call, one verified metric.

**Can angels compete with funds on dealflow?**
On speed and decisiveness, yes. On coverage, only by using structured sources that make wide screening affordable.

**Should angels use AI for deal screening?**
For extraction, normalisation and first-pass summaries, yes. Keep the thesis and the decision in human hands.

**How many deals should an angel see before investing?**
Enough that you have a basis for comparison. Fifty screened before a first cheque is a reasonable discipline.

**What is the biggest mistake angels make in syndicates?**
Assuming diligence was done by someone else. Require the memo to state what was verified and what was not.
