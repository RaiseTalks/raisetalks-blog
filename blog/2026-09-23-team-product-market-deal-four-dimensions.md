Every early-stage investment decision reduces to four questions. Who is doing this. What have they built. Who pays for it. What is the deal.

Different investors weight them differently and almost nobody writes them down, but the four dimensions are remarkably stable across firms, geographies and stages. This is what separates a 2 from a 4 in each one, and why the weakest dimension usually decides the outcome.

<!-- truncate -->

## Key takeaways

- Four dimensions: **team, product, market, deal.** Scored one to five each.
- At pre-seed, **team carries the most weight** because there is least else to underwrite. By Series A, market and deal dominate.
- **The minimum matters more than the mean.** A 5/5/5/1 loses to a 4/4/4/3 almost every time.
- Most founders over-invest in product and under-invest in deal, which is why so many warm conversations never convert.
- Every dimension is scored on your **documented record**, not on what you know.

## Team

The question: is there a specific, verifiable reason these people win this problem?

| Score | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| 1 | Names and titles only |
| 2 | Generic bios. Nothing connecting background to this problem |
| 3 | Specific verifiable backgrounds, clear roles, clear equity split |
| 4 | Plus a stated founder-problem fit and a reason this team wins |
| 5 | Plus relevant prior outcomes, named advisors, honest statement of the gap the round hires against |

**The 2 to 4 jump costs one hour.** Most teams sit at 2 not because they lack credentials but because nobody wrote the two sentences linking a background to this specific problem. "Ten years at Stripe" is a credential. "Ten years at Stripe, the last four building the reconciliation systems this product replaces" is founder-problem fit.

What moves the needle at 5: a stated weakness. A team that says "we have no enterprise sales experience, which is what the first hire out of this round is for" reads as more credible, not less. Investors are already looking for the gap. Naming it first converts a concern into evidence of self-awareness.

## Product

The question: does something exist, is its state honestly represented, and can a technical reader sanity-check it?

| Score | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| 1 | A concept |
| 2 | Something exists, described in category language rather than plainly |
| 3 | Clear plain-language description, honest shipped-vs-roadmap split |
| 4 | Plus architecture at sanity-check depth, IP ownership clear, security posture stated |
| 5 | Plus evidence of use, a defensibility argument that is not just "we are ahead" |

The cap on this dimension is almost always **overclaiming**. An investor can happily fund a thin product. They cannot fund a founder they suspect of misrepresenting one. A roadmap item described in the present tense is the most common own goal in early-stage fundraising, and it is usually accidental.

The fix is an explicit table: shipped, in build, planned. It costs nothing and it converts a credibility risk into a credibility signal.

## Market

The question: who pays, how many of them are there, and can you show your working?

| Score | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| 1 | A category name and an industry-report number |
| 2 | A defined segment, undifferentiated positioning |
| 3 | Specific buyer, derived market sizing, named competitors |
| 4 | Plus a defensible differentiation claim and evidence of demand |
| 5 | Plus a repeatable acquisition motion with known economics |

**The universal cap is a market size with no method.** If a partner cannot reconstruct your TAM from your own stated assumptions, the number is worth nothing, regardless of how large it is. Bottom-up beats top-down every time: number of target companies, times what they would pay, times an adoption assumption you can defend.

The second most common cap is refusing to name competitors. "No direct competitors" is read as "has not looked", and it is read that way in about four seconds.

## Deal

The question: is there a coherent transaction here?

| Score | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| 1 | An amount, nothing else |
| 2 | Amount and rough valuation, no use of funds |
| 3 | Amount, instrument, valuation or cap, use of funds, clean cap table |
| 4 | Plus use of funds tied to specific milestones and a runway calculation |
| 5 | Plus a stated next-round thesis: what this round proves and why that makes the next one reasonable |

**This is where founders are weakest and it is the most fixable dimension of the four.** Companies routinely sit at 2 here deep into an active process, which is exactly why so many enthusiastic conversations never convert into paper. An investor who likes you but cannot see the transaction has no decision to make.

The 5 is rarer than it should be and costs a paragraph: *"This round takes us from 12 to 50 customers and from $18K to $60K MRR, which is the threshold this category's Series A investors underwrite against."*

## Why the minimum beats the mean

The scoring instinct is to average. The decision does not work that way.

A company scoring 5/5/5/1 has a fatal flaw. Brilliant team, real product, big market, and no coherent deal - so there is nothing to say yes to. A company scoring 4/4/4/3 is entirely fundable.

So the operating rule for founders is to **work your lowest dimension, always**, not the one you find most interesting. Almost every founder's instinct is to keep improving product, which is usually already their strongest score.

And for investors: **check for the 1 before you look at the average.** A single fatal dimension is the thing an average is specifically designed to hide.

## How the weighting shifts by stage

| Stage | Team | Product | Market | Deal |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pre-seed | Heaviest | Moderate | Moderate | Light |
| Seed | Heavy | Heavy | Heavy | Moderate |
| Series A | Moderate | Moderate | Heaviest | Heavy |

At pre-seed there is little else to underwrite, so team dominates. By Series A the market has to be demonstrably large and the deal has to make mathematical sense for a fund's return model. The dimensions do not change; their weights do.

## Frequently asked questions

**What do investors look for in a startup?**
Team, product, market and deal. At pre-seed, team weighs heaviest; by Series A, market and deal do.

**What makes a startup fundable?**
No fatal weakness in any of the four dimensions, plus a coherent transaction. Strength in three dimensions does not compensate for a 1 in the fourth.

**Which dimension do founders neglect most?**
Deal. Amount, instrument, valuation, use of funds tied to milestones, and a clean cap table. It is also the fastest to fix.

**How do I know my scores?**
Have someone outside the company score your written record blind, then compare with your own scores. The gaps are the real list. Or get a [readiness score](/blog/fundraising-readiness-score-guide).
