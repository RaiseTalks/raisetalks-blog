A pitch deck is designed to be presented. It has a narrative arc, deliberate omissions, and slides that only make sense with you talking over them. It is a persuasion artefact optimised for a room you are standing in.

Most decks are read in a room you are not in. Alone, on a laptop, at 8pm, in a few minutes, by someone who will decide without asking you a single question. Under those conditions, the thing that makes a deck good makes it fail.

<!-- truncate -->

## Key takeaways

- A deck is optimised for **presentation**; most decks are consumed as **documents**.
- Narrative compression, the deck's core virtue, reads as missing information when nobody is narrating.
- Decks **drift** from your other materials. A record does not, because there is one source.
- A deck cannot be compared against other companies. A field set can.
- Keep the deck. Stop asking it to do a job it was never designed for.

## The two jobs, confused

| | Pitch deck | Structured record |
|---|---|---|
| Designed for | A room with you in it | A reader with no access to you |
| Optimises for | Persuasion and memorability | Completeness and verifiability |
| Handles omission by | Deliberate compression | Explicit fields, including empty ones |
| Comparable to other companies | No | Yes |
| Drifts from other materials | Constantly | No, regenerated from one source |
| Answers a question you did not anticipate | No | Often |

Both are necessary. The error is asking one to do the other's job, and it runs in both directions: founders who send only a deck and wonder why diligence takes six weeks, and founders who build a meticulous record and never write a story anyone remembers.

## Why compression fails without a narrator

Good deck design removes everything that is not load-bearing for the story. In a live pitch that is correct - you fill the gaps in real time, you read the room, you emphasise what that particular investor cares about.

Read cold, the same compression has a different effect. Each removed detail becomes a question the reader cannot ask. And unanswered questions do not stay neutral; they get resolved against you, because an investor with forty other companies has no incentive to give a stranger the benefit of the doubt.

A slide reading "$18K MRR, growing 14% MoM" is excellent when you are there to say it is 47 customers, mostly mid-market, with the growth coming from a channel you found in August. Alone, it raises three questions and answers none.

## The drift problem

This is the failure mode nobody plans for and everybody experiences.

By month four of a raise you have: a deck, a model, a one-pager, a data room folder, six months of investor updates and forty emails with numbers in them. Each was correct when written. None were updated together.

So the deck says 47 customers, the model assumes 52 by now, the October update said 44, and an email from last week said "about fifty". An investor who has seen three of these does not conclude that you are dishonest. They conclude you do not have a firm grip on your own numbers, which at early stage is close to the same thing in effect.

The structural fix is not discipline - discipline fails at month four of a raise, reliably. It is **a single source**: every number lives in one field, and every artefact is generated or regenerated from it.

## The comparability problem

The deeper issue is on the investor's side.

An investor comparing your deck to another company's deck is comparing two persuasion artefacts. Different slides, different metric definitions, different emphasis, different design budgets. There are no common axes.

So what actually gets compared is **how well the deck was made** - which measures design budget and founder writing ability, and correlates with company quality only loosely. Founders who are unusually good at storytelling are systematically over-rated in a deck-first process, and founders who are not are systematically under-rated. Neither is a fact about the business.

A standard field set is comparable by construction, which is what makes a consistent score possible at all. That argument in full: [you cannot compare two startups today](/blog/you-cannot-compare-two-startups).

## What each one is actually for

**The deck is for the room.** Keep it. It is the right artefact for a live pitch, a demo day, a partner meeting. Make it narrative, make it memorable, make it short.

**The record is for everything else.** The cold outreach link. The follow-up. The partner who was not in the meeting. The associate doing the first read. The investor in another timezone at 11pm. The diligence process.

The practical split:

| Moment | Send |
|---|---|
| Cold outreach | Link to the public record. Never an attachment. |
| After a first call | Record at basic tier, plus the deck if they ask |
| Live pitch | Deck |
| Partner circulation | Record. It survives being forwarded without you. |
| Diligence | Record at due-diligence tier |
| Investor updates | Generated from the record |

## The awkward implication

If the record is what gets read in every asynchronous moment, then **the record is your primary fundraising material** and the deck is the secondary one.

That inverts how most founders allocate their time. Weeks are spent on deck design; the data room is assembled in a panic when the first investor asks for one.

The reversal is not that the deck does not matter. It is that the deck matters in a small number of high-touch moments, and the record matters in every other moment - which is most of them, and all of the ones where you are not in the room to defend yourself.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do investors actually read pitch decks?**
Yes, quickly and usually alone. Which is precisely why a deck built for live presentation underperforms as a cold-read document.

**Should I send my deck or my data room first?**
A link to your public record. It answers more questions, survives forwarding, and does not require an attachment download.

**How long should a pitch deck be?**
Ten to fifteen slides. Length is not the issue; the issue is expecting it to do the record's job.

**Is a data room a replacement for a pitch deck?**
No. Different jobs. Keep both and use each where it works.

**Why do my numbers keep contradicting each other?**
Because they live in multiple documents updated at different times. The fix is one source that every artefact reads from.
